Consensus · references · hover state

Why is this source here?

One line added to the source hover card that already exists. Nothing else changes: no new panel, no new surface, no new noun. The five directions differ in where that line comes from and what it costs to produce, because at twenty sources a result set that is cheap and one that is not are different products.

Chosen · 4, plus a one-click flag

one click · optional detail

Option 4 with a way to say we got it wrong. One click on ⚑ not relevant records it and is finished. Everything after that is optional: five reasons you can tap, a free-text box, or nothing at all. The card says already recorded either way, and there is an undo at every step.

The pairing matters. Because the card already showed you the passage it picked, a bare “no” is not a vague thumbs-down — it says that passage did not justify this paper for my question, against a known query, rank, sub-query and quote. The record is specific before the user types anything, which is what makes one click enough.

forOne click is the whole requirement; detail is opt-in and never blocks. Separates right paper, wrong quote from a retrieval mistake, which are fixed in different places. againstA flag inside a hover card needs a grace period so the card survives the mouse travel, and hover is a poor home for anything on touch.

The five it came from

1 · Assembled

zero extra compute

Built by string templating from what the pipeline already emitted: which of the three sub-queries retrieved the paper, how many passages matched, whether the answer cited it.

why  Cited in the answer · 23 passages matched
      from search 2 of 3: “CRISPR off-target editing, genome-wide off-target profiling”
forFree, instant, identical every time, and impossible to hallucinate. againstMechanical. It says where the paper came from, not what it contributes.

2 · Written with the answer

~200 output tokens, on an existing call

The model that writes the answer also emits one short clause per source, in the same completion. Twenty one-liners for a couple of hundred extra output tokens on a call that was already happening.

why  Surveyed the detection standards your question asks about.
forReads like a person wrote it, and it is about the question rather than the plumbing. The cheapest way to get real language. againstWritten before anyone hovers, so most of it is never read. Adds latency to the answer, and can be wrong with no way to tell.

3 · Written on hover

one small call per hovered paper

Nothing is precomputed. Hovering asks for one sentence about this paper against this question, then caches it. Cost follows curiosity rather than result-set size.

why  Closest match to your question. It surveys detection methods for therapeutic editing and argues the standards disagree.
      written just now, for your question
forThe richest answer, and you only pay for papers someone actually looked at. On a twenty-source list that is usually three or four. againstA spinner inside a hover is a bad place to wait. Hover is a glance; 600ms is not a glance.

4 · In the paper’s own words

zero extra compute

Nothing is generated. Retrieval already scored every passage in the paper against the query, so the top-scoring one is shown verbatim.

why  “current standards do not agree on what counts as detected”
      best of 23 matched passages · the paper’s words, not ours
forFree, and the only option that cannot hallucinate, because it is quoting. It also shows the researcher the actual evidence rather than a summary of it. againstA passage is not an explanation. Sometimes the best-matching sentence is a methods aside, and length is not controllable.

5 · Points at the answer

zero extra compute

No new sentence anywhere. Hovering a reference highlights the sentence in the answer it holds up, and the card says how many.

why  ← Highlighted: the sentence this paper holds up.
forAdds no words to a dense screen, and answers “why” with something the user already read and trusts. Also the only one that says read, not cited honestly. againstSays nothing about the eight papers of twenty the answer never cites, which are exactly the ones a researcher is unsure about.
Decided: 4, with a fallback to 1 where no passage scored well. It is free, it cannot be wrong, it shows evidence rather than a summary of evidence, and it needs no new pipeline stage. 2 is the one to test against it if the quote turns out to read badly at a glance; it is the cheapest way to get language that sounds like an explanation. 3 is the one to avoid first: it puts a spinner inside a hover, which is the one place a user will not wait.

Real data throughout: six papers, three sub-queries and the funnel captured from the live CRISPR thread. Passage counts are the product’s own for Wienert (27); the other five are illustrative.
